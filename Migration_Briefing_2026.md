# Australian Net Overseas Migration: A Data-Driven Briefing
**Prepared:** September 2026 · **Data source:** ABS Net Overseas Migration (NOM) · **Period:** 2006–2025

---

## Executive Summary

The current political debate frames Australia's migration levels as an ALP policy failure. The ABS data tells a more complicated story. While net migration has been higher under the current ALP government, the headline numbers are substantially explained by a post-COVID mathematical rebound — a structural phenomenon, not a policy choice. The visa categories now targeted by opposition proposals (students, skilled workers) were growing steadily under the previous LNP government and would have continued to grow had COVID not intervened.

---

## 1. The Headline Numbers

| Period | Government | Years | Avg Net Migration/Year (raw) | Avg Net Migration/Year (COVID-adj) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2006 | LNP | 1 yr | 107,570 | 107,570 |
| 2007–2012 | ALP | 6 yrs | 237,525 | 237,525 |
| 2013–2021 | LNP | 9 yrs | 157,011 | 192,675 |
| 2022–2025 | ALP | 4 yrs | 399,973 | 307,635 |

**Key observation:** When the COVID distortion is removed (by averaging 2020–2023 across those four years to reflect what would have occurred absent closed borders), the ALP COVID-adjusted average falls to ~308k/year and the LNP COVID-adjusted average rises to ~193k/year. The gap narrows substantially — from 2.4× to 1.6×.

---

## 2. The COVID Distortion Explained

- Australian borders were closed in **March 2020 under the LNP government**
- Borders partially reopened in **December 2021, still under LNP**
- The result: net migration in 2020 was **-4,960** and in 2021 was just **+9,300** — two years of near-zero migration representing a ~400,000 person deficit relative to trend
- When borders reopened, a structural backlog cleared — students who had deferred enrolments, skilled workers whose visas had lapsed, working holiday makers who had been locked out
- This rebound (2022: +437,850 / 2023: +530,620) occurred under ALP but was **a direct consequence of LNP-era border closure**, not new policy settings

**Analogy:** Closing a dam for two years then removing the gates does not make the downstream government responsible for the flood.

---

## 3. What Is Actually Driving Migration?

### Student Visas — the dominant category

Student visas account for **1.65 million net arrivals** over the 20-year period — the single largest category.

**However:** Student net migration was growing *consistently under the LNP*:

| Year | Government | Student Net |
|---|---|---|
| 2013 | LNP | 55,860 |
| 2015 | LNP | 82,300 |
| 2016 | LNP | 92,480 |
| 2017 | LNP | 100,200 |
| 2018 | LNP | **106,720** |
| 2019 | LNP | 51,840 *(pre-COVID slowdown)* |
| 2020 | LNP | -32,520 *(COVID)* |
| 2021 | LNP | -8,230 *(COVID)* |
| 2022 | ALP | 221,690 *(rebound)* |
| 2023 | ALP | 241,720 *(rebound)* |
| 2024 | ALP | 118,810 |
| 2025 | ALP | 106,870 |

**Note:** By 2024–2025, student net migration has returned to levels broadly consistent with the 2017–2018 LNP trajectory. The 2022–2023 spike is a rebound, not a new baseline.

If the COVID years (2020–2023) are averaged (~106k/year), the student visa trend line is essentially continuous from 2013 to 2025, crossing government boundaries without discontinuity.

### Permanent Migration — stable regardless of government

Permanent skilled migration has run at **~28,000–38,000/year** consistently across both governments. Permanent family migration runs at **~18,000–31,000/year**. These are relatively modest and stable numbers — far below the levels that dominate public discourse.

### Australian Citizens — net negative

In most years, more Australian citizens *leave* Australia than return. The cumulative net is **-206,500** over the 20-year period. This is not discussed in the migration debate.

## 4. The Policy Proposals and Their Implications

### Proposal: Stop student migration

This is the most significant proposal by quantum. Student migration drives substantial economic activity:

- International students contributed approximately **$36–40 billion** to the Australian economy in 2023 (Universities Australia)
- Universities, TAFEs and private colleges are heavily dependent on international student fee revenue
- Many student visa holders work in sectors with labour shortages (hospitality, retail, aged care)
- A blanket stop would devastate the higher education sector and likely cause several regional universities to become unviable
- **GDP impact is rarely quantified in public debate** — this is a significant omission

The data shows student migration would have continued growing under any government absent COVID. It reflects global demand for Australian education, not a policy failure.

### Proposal: Stop family migration for students

This affects secondary visa holders (spouses/dependants of student visa holders). This is a more targeted measure and was in fact tightened by the ALP government in 2023–2024. The data shows this is a relatively small contributor to net migration.

### Proposal: Cut skilled migration

Permanent skilled migration is approximately 28,000–38,000/year — a modest number relative to overall flows. Temporary skilled migration (sponsored workers) is similarly modest at ~15,000–45,000/year. Australia has documented skill shortages in engineering, healthcare, construction and technology. Cutting skilled migration would slow the very sectors needed to build housing supply.

**The paradox:** Cutting skilled construction and trade workers to address a housing shortage is counterproductive. The housing supply problem requires more builders, not fewer.

---

## 5. What the Data Does Not Show

The ABS NOM data does not directly address:

- **Housing demand vs supply:** The relationship between migration and housing affordability is real but mediated by planning systems, construction capacity, and investor behaviour — all of which have been policy-constrained independently of migration
- **Inflation:** The RBA has noted that post-COVID inflation was globally driven (supply chain, energy costs) and that Australia's experience tracked comparable economies regardless of migration settings
- **Fiscal contribution:** Migrants — particularly skilled and student visa holders — are typically net fiscal contributors. Full cost-benefit analysis is absent from public debate

---

## 6. Summary: What the Data Actually Shows

1. **Net migration is higher under ALP** — this is factually correct
2. **The headline gap is largely a COVID rebound** — removing the COVID distortion narrows the gap from 2.4× to 1.6× and shows trend continuity across governments
3. **Student visas are the biggest driver** — and were on a strong growth trajectory under LNP before COVID interrupted them
4. **Permanent migration is stable** — it has not surged and is broadly bipartisan in magnitude
5. **The proposed cuts target categories with high economic value** and the GDP cost is not being discussed
6. **The housing and inflation problems have structural causes** that migration alone cannot explain, and cutting skilled migration may worsen the housing supply constraint

---

## Recommended Questions for Policy Discussion

1. What is the independent economic modelling of GDP impact from a student visa cap?
2. What would the COVID-adjusted migration trend look like under a continuation of LNP settings from 2022?
3. What proportion of net migration is temporary (and self-reversing) versus permanent?
4. What is the net fiscal contribution of each visa category?
5. If housing supply is the problem, why are skilled trades and construction workers being targeted for cuts?
