Australian Migration 2006–2025
A Data-Driven Policy Briefing

Analysis of 20 years of ABS Net Overseas Migration (NOM) data, disaggregated by visa type and mapped to federal governing party. Prepared to inform debate on proposed migration policy changes.

📊 Source: ABS NOM 2006–2025 🗓️ Prepared: September 2026 🇦🇺 National level data 📈 20-year dataset

Executive Summary

The current political debate frames Australia's migration levels as an ALP policy failure. The ABS data tells a more complicated story. While net migration has been higher under the current ALP government, the headline numbers are substantially explained by a post-COVID mathematical rebound — a structural phenomenon, not a policy choice. The visa categories targeted by opposition proposals (students, skilled workers) were growing steadily under the previous LNP government and would have continued absent COVID. When the COVID distortion is removed, the gap between ALP and LNP migration averages narrows from 2.4× to 1.6× — and the student visa trend shows effective continuity across the two governments.

1 — Methodological Notes: What the Data Actually Measures
📐

The NZ Subclass 444 Measurement Problem

Why raw arrival counts overstate New Zealand citizen migration flows

Around 670,000 New Zealand citizens are estimated to live in Australia (DFAT current estimate; ABS records approximately 638,000 New Zealand-born usual residents at June 2025 — a related but distinct concept). The Special Category Visa (Subclass 444) is normally granted each time an eligible New Zealand citizen enters Australia and ceases when the holder leaves.

Consequently, border-arrival or visa-grant counts can record the same individual repeatedly across multiple trans-Tasman trips. This is an instance of the broader distinction emphasised by the ABS: Overseas Arrivals and Departures data measure border movements rather than migrants, and should not be treated as a measure of population change. Net Overseas Migration, which applies the 12/16-month residence rule, is the appropriate measure of migration.

Implication: Analysts who work with raw arrival counts without adjusting for this effect will overstate temporary migration flows. The NZ 444 net migration figures in this briefing use NOM methodology, which correctly counts each person once based on their 12/16-month residence behaviour — not their number of border crossings.
🔄

Bridging Visas and Onshore Transitions

Why pipeline accumulation is structural, not a compliance failure

When a student visa holder applies onshore for a Temporary Graduate, skilled, or permanent residence visa, they receive a Bridging Visa maintaining lawful status. Any departure during this period is recorded against the Bridging Visa, not the student visa.

This means a substantial volume of “departures” from the student visa category are administratively invisible: the person transitions to another visa without generating a departure record against their student visa. The net accumulation of 1.25M+ in temporary student visa flows since 2011 (ABS Tablebuilder) should therefore be read as a pipeline depth indicator, not a count of persons unlawfully remaining.

Key insight: Volume caps on student visa arrivals will reduce future cohorts but will not resolve the existing stock for many years. The stock is a structural feature of the pipeline design.
2 — The COVID Distortion
🦠

The Post-COVID Rebound Is Not a Policy Choice

Understanding why 2022–2023 migration appears anomalously high

Australian borders were closed in March 2020 under the LNP government and only partially reopened in December 2021 — also under LNP. The result was two years of near-zero migration, creating a structural deficit of approximately 400,000 persons relative to trend:

  • 📉2020 net migration: −4,960 — borders closed (LNP)
  • 📉2021 net migration: +9,300 — borders still largely closed (LNP)
  • 📈2022 net migration: +437,850 — structural backlog clearing (ALP)
  • 📈2023 net migration: +530,620 — peak rebound year (ALP)
  • 📉2024 net migration: +330,460 — rebound already subsiding (ALP)
  • 📉2025 net migration: +300,960 — continuing to normalise (ALP)
The dam analogy: Closing a dam for two years then removing the gates does not make the downstream government responsible for the flood. The 2022–23 surge was students who had deferred enrolments, skilled workers whose visas had lapsed, and working holiday makers who had been locked out — all clearing simultaneously once borders reopened.
COVID-adjusted comparison: When 2020–2023 are averaged to reflect what would have occurred without border closure, the ALP average falls from 400k to ~278k/year and the LNP average rises from 157k to ~193k/year. The gap narrows from 2.4× to 1.6×.

4 — What Is Actually Driving Migration?
🎓

📊

Permanent Migration — Stable Across Both Governments

The categories most targeted by opposition proposals are the most stable

Permanent migration has been broadly bipartisan and stable for 20 years:

  • 🟢Permanent Skilled: ~28,000–38,000/year under both governments. Total 20-yr net: 611,000.
  • 🟢Permanent Family: ~18,000–31,000/year. Total 20-yr net: 455,000. Modestly higher under early ALP.
  • 🟡Permanent Humanitarian: ~5,900–22,500/year. Volatile — driven by global refugee events, not domestic policy settings alone.

The temporary categories — student, visitor, working holiday, temporary skilled — drive virtually all headline volatility. Permanent migration has not surged.

Key insight: Opposition proposals to cut permanent skilled and family migration would barely move the overall headline number, while removing economically productive migrants.

One rarely-cited data point: Australian citizens are net negative in most years. Cumulative 20-year net: −206,500. More Australians have left than returned — nobody is proposing to address this.

5 — Assessment of Current Policy Proposals
🚨

Stop Student Migration

Highest impact proposal — also highest cost

  • ❌International students contributed ~$36–40 billion to the Australian economy in 2023 (Universities Australia)
  • ❌Multiple regional universities would become financially unviable
  • ❌Student visa holders fill critical labour shortages: hospitality, aged care, retail
  • ⚠️The GDP impact of a student cap is not being publicly quantified — a significant omission from the debate
  • 📊Growth in student numbers was on the same trajectory under LNP (2013–2018) — this reflects global demand, not a policy failure
⚠️

Stop Family Migration for Students

More targeted — already being tightened

  • ✅More targeted than a student cap — affects secondary visa holders (spouses/dependants) only
  • ✅ALP government already tightened this in 2023–2024
  • 📊Secondary visa holders are a smaller contributor to net migration than primary holders
  • ⚠️May reduce Australia's attractiveness as a study destination relative to UK, Canada and USA, which allow family accompaniment
🤔

Cut Skilled Migration

Counterproductive given current environment

  • 📊Permanent skilled: ~28–38k/year — modest relative to overall flows
  • 📊Temporary skilled workers: ~15–45k/year net
  • ❌Australia has documented shortages in engineering, healthcare, construction and technology
  • 🚨The housing paradox: cutting skilled construction and trade workers to address a housing shortage is directly counterproductive. More builders are needed, not fewer.
6 — What the Data Does Not Show
🏠

Housing Affordability

Migration is a contributing factor — not the primary cause

The relationship between migration and housing affordability is real but mediated by:

  • 🏗️Planning systems that restrict density and slow approvals
  • 💰Investor behaviour and negative gearing policy
  • 📉Construction capacity constraints — labour and materials shortages
  • 📊Undersupply predates the recent migration surge by a decade
Cutting skilled migration — particularly construction trades — reduces the workforce needed to build the housing required to address the affordability crisis. The policy is internally contradictory.
📈

Inflation & Fiscal Contribution

Two dimensions absent from public debate

  • 🌍Inflation: The RBA noted post-COVID inflation was globally driven — supply chain disruption, energy costs. Australia tracked comparable economies regardless of migration settings.
  • 💼Fiscal contribution: Skilled and student visa holders are typically net fiscal contributors — paying taxes, HECS debts, GST — with lower claims on welfare and aged care in the short term.
  • 📊Productivity: Migrants fill roles in sectors with documented shortages, supporting GDP output that would otherwise be lost.
  • ⚠️Full cost-benefit analysis by visa category is absent from the current public debate.
7 — Summary: What the Data Actually Shows
✅

Six Key Findings

Evidence-based conclusions from 20 years of ABS NOM data

  • 1️⃣Net migration is higher under ALP — this is factually correct and uncontested.
  • 2️⃣The headline gap is largely a COVID rebound. Removing the COVID distortion narrows the gap from 2.4× to 1.6× and shows trend continuity across governments.
  • 3️⃣Student visas are the biggest driver — and were on a strong growth trajectory under LNP before COVID. By 2024–25 student net migration has returned to LNP-era levels.
  • 4️⃣Permanent migration is stable — it has not surged and is broadly bipartisan in magnitude. Proposed cuts would minimally affect headline numbers while removing economically valuable migrants.
  • 5️⃣The proposed cuts target high-economic-value categories and the GDP cost is not being publicly modelled or discussed.
  • 6️⃣Housing and inflation have structural causes that migration alone cannot explain — and cutting skilled migration may worsen, not improve, the housing supply constraint.

9 — Policy Proposals: A Ranked Assessment
✅

1. Offshore PR Processing

High tractability — strongest international precedent

Require permanent residence applications by former student and Temporary Graduate (485) visa holders to be lodged offshore, following a genuine departure. Currently, onshore PR conversion is the dominant pathway and the primary mechanism generating pipeline accumulation. US, UK, and Canada all require offshore processing for equivalent categories.

Primarily an administrative change — does not require legislative amendment for most visa categories. A 24-month transition period with hardship exemptions is appropriate.
🎓

2. Field-of-Study Gate on Subclass 485

Medium tractability — modelled on Canada’s PGWP

Restrict 485 eligibility to graduates from fields aligned with Ministerial Directions 121/122 priorities: healthcare, construction and trades, education, STEM, primary industries. Graduates outside priority fields receive a shorter post-study work period (12 months maximum) without onshore PR conversion rights.

Acts as an upstream signal to prospective students and agents, redirecting enrolment toward priority areas without imposing blunt volume caps.
🔬

3. STEM Post-Study Extension

Medium tractability — additional 24 months for qualifying STEM graduates

A STEM-specific stream within the 485 framework (analogous to US STEM OPT) would attract a different student profile and signal that Australia competes seriously for STEM talent. Eligibility should be identified via ASCED Field of Education classifications and CRICOS course codes — the administrative architecture that already governs Australia’s education system and was used for the previous extended post-study work scheme. ANZSRC Fields of Research may be referenced for conceptual alignment but should not be the operational classifier, as ANZSRC classifies research activity rather than taught degree programs.

Employer registration requirements (analogous to US E-Verify for STEM OPT) should apply to prevent credential-holder warehousing.
📊

4. Volume Caps + Quality Thresholds

Medium tractability — caps alone are insufficient

Caps without quality thresholds simply shift demand within the eligible pool without addressing underlying incentive structures. If caps are implemented, I propose combining them with:

  • 📌Minimum course level: AQF Level 5 (no new student visas below this except ELICOS and specific pathway programs) — proposed threshold, not current law
  • 📌Minimum English: IELTS 6.5 (or equivalent) for undergraduate and above — proposed; current floor is IELTS 6.0
  • 📌Rigorous application of the Genuine Student (GS) requirement, which replaced the former GTE test on 23 March 2024
TEQSA and ASQA should be resourced to audit GS interview quality and agent compliance, with provider deregistration used as a genuine deterrent.
10 — Recommended Questions for Policy Discussion
❓

Questions for Parliament and Policy Makers

Gaps in the current public debate that warrant independent economic modelling

  1. What is the independent economic modelling of GDP impact from a student visa cap — including university sector viability, labour supply, and flow-on consumer spending effects?
  2. What would the COVID-adjusted migration trend look like under a continuation of LNP settings from 2022? Is the current ALP trend materially different from what would have occurred under the previous government?
  3. What proportion of net migration is temporary (and self-reversing over time) versus permanent? Are we conflating a short-term rebound with a structural shift?
  4. What is the net fiscal contribution (taxes paid vs services consumed) per visa category? Which categories are net fiscal positives?
  5. If housing supply is the problem, how does reducing skilled trades and construction workers improve the situation? What is the net effect on housing completions per year?
9 — Primary vs Secondary Student Visas
👨‍👩‍👧

Primary vs Secondary (Dependant) Student Visa Net Migration 2011–2025

Australia has historically been the most permissive Anglophone destination on secondary visa entitlements. International comparison and 2024 reform context.

Secondary student visa holders are spouses and dependent children accompanying a primary student. Australia's historical permissiveness in this area — granting unrestricted work rights to secondary holders whose primary student was enrolled at master's level or above — has been a structural attractor for household units seeking dual incomes through the education migration pathway. The entitlement turns on the distinction between coursework and research degrees: for a master's by coursework or master's (extended), the dependant may work without restriction while the course is in session, but the primary student remains capped at 48 hours per fortnight; for a master's by research or doctorate, both primary student and dependants may exceed 48 hours.

The disconnect is empirically clear: The countries most frequently cited in political discourse about social cohesion and crime — Somalia, Lebanon, Iran — do not appear as significant contributors to student visa growth. Policy interventions calibrated to the political narrative rather than the data will be poorly targeted, simultaneously too broad (affecting large legitimate education cohorts) and too narrow (not addressing the stated concerns).

Why are the surge countries growing?

  • 🗣️English-medium education or strong English uptake. The Philippines has English-medium tertiary education. Bangladesh, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka retain English from British colonial systems. Brazil and Colombia have seen rapid English expansion via outsourcing and technology sectors. Kenya has one of the highest English literacy rates in sub-Saharan Africa.
  • 🤝Active Australian education agent networks post-2010. Following the Bradley Review and recovery from the 2009–10 student welfare crisis, agent networks expanded commercially into new source markets. The countries showing rapid growth are those where Australian agent presence expanded in 2012–2018.
  • 📉Weak local graduate employment markets. Nepal, Bangladesh, and Pakistan all have graduate unemployment and underemployment rates that make the Australian student visa financially rational even accounting for financing costs. The "student visa as migration vehicle" is a rational calculation when local alternatives are worse.
  • 🌐Diaspora network effects. Once a critical mass of a nationality's citizens establishes itself in Australia, information asymmetry about the migration pathway falls sharply. Each cohort facilitates the next — diaspora accumulation reduces perceived risk and lowers transaction costs for subsequent migrants.
11 — The Accumulation Problem
📦

1.25M+ Net Stock: A Pipeline Indicator, Not a Compliance Metric

Why the student visa stock has accumulated — and what structural reform would actually address it

The net student visa stock has accumulated to over 1.25 million since 2011 (ABS Tablebuilder). This figure generates significant political anxiety but is routinely misinterpreted. It does not primarily reflect overstaying or non-compliance. It reflects the deliberate design of Australia's multi-stage visa transition pipeline.

The pipeline mechanics: When a student visa holder transitions onshore to a Temporary Graduate (485) visa, then to a Temporary Skill Shortage (482) visa, and eventually to permanent residence — no departure is ever recorded against the student visa category. Each step forward through the pipeline is administratively invisible as a departure. The 1.25M+ figure counts everyone who entered on a student visa and has not yet physically departed or been recorded as a PR grant against the student category.

Five structural reasons for accumulation:

  • 1️⃣Visa hopping pipeline. Student → Temporary Graduate (485) → Skilled/Employer Sponsored → PR. Each stage is an onshore transition with no departure required. The person moves through the pipeline but never exits the NOM stock.
  • 2️⃣PR grants don't clear the student stock counter. Once PR is granted, no departure is recorded against the student visa category. The person has "succeeded" through the pipeline but remains counted in the accumulation figure.
  • 3️⃣PR transition rates are higher than commonly cited. The often-cited 16% figure originates in the 2018 Shaping a Nation study (Treasury/Home Affairs), which examined 1.6 million international students from 2000–01 to 2013–14. More recent longitudinal evidence is substantially higher: Treasury's analysis of the 2006–07 student cohort found 39% had obtained PR after approximately a decade; Jobs and Skills Australia reports that roughly 45% of the 2010–11 higher-education cohort had obtained PR or Australian citizenship by 2021–22 (the VET equivalent was 56%). The differences partly reflect cohort definitions, methodology, and observation periods. JSA also finds that those who reach PR typically pass through two or three consecutive temporary visas, with higher-education graduates taking a median of approximately 4.7 years after graduation to obtain PR.
  • 4️⃣The "permanently temporary" cohort. The Grattan Institute's "graduates in limbo" framing captures a real phenomenon: a large cohort cycling through successive bridging visas and temporary work categories, accumulating the points, experience, or employer relationships needed to qualify for PR. This cohort has genuine claims on policy attention — not because they are non-compliant, but because prolonged limbo creates adverse labour market outcomes and wage exploitation risk.
  • 5️⃣Bridging visas and invisible transitions. When a student applies onshore for a PR visa, they receive a Bridging Visa maintaining lawful status. Any departure during this period is recorded against the Bridging Visa, not the student visa. The student visa stock does not decrease even if the person briefly departs.
The most effective structural reform: Requiring offshore lodgement for permanent residence applications by former student and Temporary Graduate visa holders — consistent with practice in the United States, United Kingdom, and Canada — would force a genuine departure decision at the PR conversion stage, reduce pipeline accumulation over time, and align Australia with international norms. This is primarily an administrative change and does not require legislative amendment for most visa categories. Volume caps on new student arrivals will reduce future cohorts but will not resolve the existing 1.25M+ stock for many years.

Note: The accumulation figure should be read as a pipeline indicator only. It is not evidence of non-compliance. Any policy framing that treats the 1.25M+ figure as a "problem" of illegal overstaying will misdirect enforcement resources and produce politically visible but analytically empty results.
