Executive Summary

The current political debate frames Australia's migration levels as an ALP policy failure. The ABS data tells a more complicated story. While net migration has been higher under the current ALP government, the headline numbers are substantially explained by a post-COVID mathematical rebound — a structural phenomenon, not a policy choice. The visa categories targeted by opposition proposals (students, skilled workers) were growing steadily under the previous LNP government and would have continued absent COVID. When the COVID distortion is removed, the gap between ALP and LNP migration averages narrows from 2.4× to 1.6× — and the student visa trend shows effective continuity across the two governments.

1 — The COVID Distortion
🦠

The Post-COVID Rebound Is Not a Policy Choice

Understanding why 2022–2023 migration appears anomalously high

Australian borders were closed in March 2020 under the LNP government and only partially reopened in December 2021 — also under LNP. The result was two years of near-zero migration, creating a structural deficit of approximately 400,000 persons relative to trend:

  • 📉2020 net migration: −4,960 — borders closed (LNP)
  • 📉2021 net migration: +9,300 — borders still largely closed (LNP)
  • 📈2022 net migration: +437,850 — structural backlog clearing (ALP)
  • 📈2023 net migration: +530,620 — peak rebound year (ALP)
  • 📉2024 net migration: +330,460 — rebound already subsiding (ALP)
  • 📉2025 net migration: +300,960 — continuing to normalise (ALP)
The dam analogy: Closing a dam for two years then removing the gates does not make the downstream government responsible for the flood. The 2022–23 surge was students who had deferred enrolments, skilled workers whose visas had lapsed, and working holiday makers who had been locked out — all clearing simultaneously once borders reopened.
COVID-adjusted comparison: When 2020–2023 are averaged to reflect what would have occurred without border closure, the ALP average falls from 400k to ~278k/year and the LNP average rises from 157k to ~193k/year. The gap narrows from 2.4× to 1.6×.

3 — What Is Actually Driving Migration?
🎓

Student Visas — The Dominant Category

By 2024–2025, student net migration has returned to levels broadly consistent with the 2017–2018 LNP trajectory. If 2020–2023 are averaged (~106k/year), the trend line is effectively continuous across both governments.
📊

Permanent Migration — Stable Across Both Governments

The categories most targeted by opposition proposals are the most stable

Permanent migration has been broadly bipartisan and stable for 20 years:

  • 🟢Permanent Skilled: ~28,000–38,000/year under both governments. Total 20-yr net: 611,000.
  • 🟢Permanent Family: ~18,000–31,000/year. Total 20-yr net: 455,000. Modestly higher under early ALP.
  • 🟡Permanent Humanitarian: ~5,900–22,500/year. Volatile — driven by global refugee events, not domestic policy settings alone.

The temporary categories — student, visitor, working holiday, temporary skilled — drive virtually all headline volatility. Permanent migration has not surged.

Key insight: Opposition proposals to cut permanent skilled and family migration would barely move the overall headline number, while removing economically productive migrants.

One rarely-cited data point: Australian citizens are net negative in most years. Cumulative 20-year net: −206,500. More Australians have left than returned — nobody is proposing to address this.

4 — Assessment of Current Policy Proposals
🚨

Stop Student Migration

Highest impact proposal — also highest cost

  • ❌International students contributed ~$36–40 billion to the Australian economy in 2023 (Universities Australia)
  • ❌Multiple regional universities would become financially unviable
  • ❌Student visa holders fill critical labour shortages: hospitality, aged care, retail
  • ⚠️The GDP impact of a student cap is not being publicly quantified — a significant omission from the debate
  • 📊Growth in student numbers was on the same trajectory under LNP (2013–2018) — this reflects global demand, not a policy failure
⚠️

Stop Family Migration for Students

More targeted — already being tightened

  • ✅More targeted than a student cap — affects secondary visa holders (spouses/dependants) only
  • ✅ALP government already tightened this in 2023–2024
  • 📊Secondary visa holders are a smaller contributor to net migration than primary holders
  • ⚠️May reduce Australia's attractiveness as a study destination relative to UK, Canada and USA, which allow family accompaniment
🤔

Cut Skilled Migration

Counterproductive given current environment

  • 📊Permanent skilled: ~28–38k/year — modest relative to overall flows
  • 📊Temporary skilled workers: ~15–45k/year net
  • ❌Australia has documented shortages in engineering, healthcare, construction and technology
  • 🚨The housing paradox: cutting skilled construction and trade workers to address a housing shortage is directly counterproductive. More builders are needed, not fewer.
5 — What the Data Does Not Show
🏠

Housing Affordability

Migration is a contributing factor — not the primary cause

The relationship between migration and housing affordability is real but mediated by:

  • 🏗️Planning systems that restrict density and slow approvals
  • 💰Investor behaviour and negative gearing policy
  • 📉Construction capacity constraints — labour and materials shortages
  • 📊Undersupply predates the recent migration surge by a decade
Cutting skilled migration — particularly construction trades — reduces the workforce needed to build the housing required to address the affordability crisis. The policy is internally contradictory.
📈

Inflation & Fiscal Contribution

Two dimensions absent from public debate

  • 🌍Inflation: The RBA noted post-COVID inflation was globally driven — supply chain disruption, energy costs. Australia tracked comparable economies regardless of migration settings.
  • 💼Fiscal contribution: Skilled and student visa holders are typically net fiscal contributors — paying taxes, HECS debts, GST — with lower claims on welfare and aged care in the short term.
  • 📊Productivity: Migrants fill roles in sectors with documented shortages, supporting GDP output that would otherwise be lost.
  • ⚠️Full cost-benefit analysis by visa category is absent from the current public debate.
6 — Summary: What the Data Actually Shows
✅

Six Key Findings

Evidence-based conclusions from 20 years of ABS NOM data

  • 1️⃣Net migration is higher under ALP — this is factually correct and uncontested.
  • 2️⃣The headline gap is largely a COVID rebound. Removing the COVID distortion narrows the gap from 2.4× to 1.6× and shows trend continuity across governments.
  • 3️⃣Student visas are the biggest driver — and were on a strong growth trajectory under LNP before COVID. By 2024–25 student net migration has returned to LNP-era levels.
  • 4️⃣Permanent migration is stable — it has not surged and is broadly bipartisan in magnitude. Proposed cuts would minimally affect headline numbers while removing economically valuable migrants.
  • 5️⃣The proposed cuts target high-economic-value categories and the GDP cost is not being publicly modelled or discussed.
  • 6️⃣Housing and inflation have structural causes that migration alone cannot explain — and cutting skilled migration may worsen, not improve, the housing supply constraint.
7 — Recommended Questions for Policy Discussion
❓

Questions for Parliament and Policy Makers

Gaps in the current public debate that warrant independent economic modelling

  1. What is the independent economic modelling of GDP impact from a student visa cap — including university sector viability, labour supply, and flow-on consumer spending effects?
  2. What would the COVID-adjusted migration trend look like under a continuation of LNP settings from 2022? Is the current ALP trend materially different from what would have occurred under the previous government?
  3. What proportion of net migration is temporary (and self-reversing over time) versus permanent? Are we conflating a short-term rebound with a structural shift?
  4. What is the net fiscal contribution (taxes paid vs services consumed) per visa category? Which categories are net fiscal positives?
  5. If housing supply is the problem, how does reducing skilled trades and construction workers improve the situation? What is the net effect on housing completions per year?
